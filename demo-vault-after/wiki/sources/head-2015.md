# Head et al. (2015), The extent and consequences of p-hacking in science

Head, Holman, Lanfear, Kahn and Jennions. PLoS Biology 13(3), e1002106, published 13 March 2015 as a Perspective article. The authors are evolutionary biologists at the Australian National University and Macquarie University. Data and R code are deposited in Dryad (doi 10.5061/dryad.79d43). The supplementary S1 Text, which describes how the data were collected, is not part of the extracted source, so details of the text-mining pipeline beyond what the main text states are not available here.

## What the paper set out to do

The paper asks two questions. First, how widespread is p-hacking across the scientific literature. Second, does p-hacking distort the conclusions drawn from meta-analyses. It answers both by examining p-curves, the distribution of reported p-values for a set of studies.

Two datasets are used. The first is p-values text-mined from all open-access papers in PubMed, analysed by discipline. The second is p-values from the primary studies that fed twelve published meta-analyses on sexual selection in evolutionary biology.

## How the paper defines p-hacking

The paper distinguishes two types of researcher-driven publication bias. Selection, or the file drawer effect, is the lower publication rate of studies with non-significant results. Inflation bias, which the paper also calls p-hacking or selective reporting, is the misreporting of true effect sizes in published studies. It occurs when researchers try out several statistical analyses or data eligibility specifications and selectively report those that produce significant results.

Practices the paper names as leading to p-hacking: analysing data midway through an experiment to decide whether to continue collecting; recording many response variables and deciding which to report after analysis; deciding whether to include or drop outliers after analysis; excluding, combining or splitting treatment groups after analysis; including or excluding covariates after analysis; and stopping data exploration once an analysis yields a significant p-value. In the meta-analysis section it adds that reporting a non-significant result as "p < 0.05" is a component of p-hacking that should not be ignored.

The paper's account of why this happens is the incentive to publish statistically significant results, which it attributes to journals disproportionately publishing significant findings and to employers and funders counting papers weighted by journal impact factor.

## How the paper reads a p-curve

When the true effect size is zero, every p-value is equally likely and the expected p-curve is flat. When the true effect is non-zero, the expected p-curve is right skewed, with more very small p-values than moderately small ones, and the skew grows with the effect size. The paper calls a right skew in the significant range evidence of "evidential value", meaning that the published evidence suggests the effect size is non-zero.

A drop in the frequency of p-values just above 0.05 is taken in earlier work as evidence of publication bias, but the paper stresses that a discontinuity at 0.05 does not distinguish selective publication from p-hacking. The signature it attributes to p-hacking alone is an overabundance of p-values just below 0.05. If researchers p-hack when there is no true effect, the p-curve shifts from flat to left skewed. If they p-hack when there is a true effect, the p-curve stays right skewed but gains a bump in the tail just below 0.05. The exact shape depends on both the true effect and the intensity of p-hacking.

### The tests

Evidential value was tested with a two-tailed sign test comparing the number of p-values in the bin 0 to 0.025 against the bin 0.025 to 0.05. Equal counts are expected under no evidential value; significantly more in the lower bin indicates a right skew, and significantly more in the upper bin would indicate severe p-hacking. The paper describes this as a slight modification of a test proposed by Simonsohn and colleagues.

The paper argues that this test is insensitive to modest p-hacking, especially when true effects are strong, because the right skew masks the left skew that p-hacking adds. Its more sensitive test for p-hacking is a one-tailed sign test asking whether the number of p-values in the bin 0.045 < p < 0.05 exceeds the number in the adjacent bin 0.04 < p < 0.045. The null hypothesis is that p-values just below 0.05 are uniform or right skewed. The bin width of 0.005 is described as a trade-off: narrower bins are more sensitive but reduce sample size. The upper cutoff is p < 0.05 rather than p = 0.05 because the authors suspect many researchers do not regard p = 0.05 as significant. The strength of p-hacking is reported as the proportion of p-values in the upper bin, with a Clopper-Pearson 95% confidence interval.

Tests were run separately for each discipline and each meta-analysis dataset. Overall tests pooled disciplines (or datasets) with a binomial generalised linear model on the proportion in the upper bin, testing whether the intercept differed from 0.5, which the paper describes as equivalent to a meta-analysis that weights each discipline by its sample size.

## Study 1: text-mined p-values across disciplines

### Data

P-values were text-mined from all open-access papers in the PubMed database. P-values from Results sections and p-values from Abstracts were analysed separately. Bin counts are the mean over 1,000 bootstraps that drew one p-value per Results section (or per Abstract), rounded to the nearest whole number. Disciplines with fewer than 50 p-values below 0.05 were excluded: eight were excluded from the Results analysis, leaving 14 disciplines, and twelve from the Abstract analysis, leaving 10. The paper notes that text-mining has recognised weaknesses for studying publication bias and says it adopted several counter-measures, described in the S1 Text.

### Evidential value

Pooling across disciplines, the estimated proportion of p-values in the upper evidential-value bin (0.025 to 0.05) was 0.257 (95% CI 0.254 to 0.259), p < 0.001, n = 14 disciplines, for Results sections, and 0.262 (0.257 to 0.267), p < 0.001, n = 10 disciplines, for Abstracts. Every discipline in both analyses showed significant evidential value (p < 0.001 in each), which the paper reads as researchers predominantly studying phenomena with non-zero effect sizes. The largest contributors were Medical and health sciences (Results: 45,460 p-values in 0 to 0.025 against 16,537 in 0.025 to 0.05), Multidisciplinary (21,209 against 6,793) and Biological sciences (11,074 against 3,562).

### P-hacking

Pooling across disciplines, the estimated proportion of p-values in the upper p-hacking bin (0.045 < p < 0.05) was 0.546 (one-tailed lower CI 0.536), p < 0.001, n = 14 disciplines, for Results sections, and 0.537 (lower CI 0.518), p < 0.001, n = 10 disciplines, for Abstracts.

By discipline, using Results-section p-values (Table 1; counts are the lower bin 0.04 to 0.045 against the upper bin 0.045 to 0.05, one-tailed binomial p):

- Medical and health sciences: 1,477 against 1,785, p < 0.001
- Biological sciences: 350 against 423, p = 0.005
- Multidisciplinary: 638 against 750, p = 0.001
- Psychology and cognitive sciences: 29 against 50, p = 0.012
- Earth sciences: 0 against 4, p = 0.063
- Environmental sciences: 10 against 19, p = 0.068
- Information and computing sciences: 20 against 30, p = 0.101
- Agricultural and veterinary sciences: 10 against 16, p = 0.163
- Chemical sciences: 14 against 17, p = 0.360
- Technology: 3 against 3, p = 0.656
- Education: 9 against 8, p = 0.685
- Engineering: 16 against 12, p = 0.828
- Studies in human society: 8 against 3, p = 0.967
- Mathematical sciences: 3 against 0, p = 1.000

The paper summarises this as more p-values in the upper bin in most disciplines, and a significant difference in every discipline with good statistical power, which it names as Medical and health sciences, Biological sciences and Multidisciplinary. Using Abstract p-values (Table 2), only Multidisciplinary (123 against 174, p = 0.002) and Information and computing sciences (5 against 15, p = 0.021) were individually significant; Medical and health sciences was 633 against 692, p = 0.056.

The paper contrasts its result with a text-mining study of five medical journals (Jager and Leek, 2014) that it says found no evidence of p-hacking, and repeats the criticism that Abstract p-values are optional and so likely to be censored towards the strongest results. On the paper's account such censoring would exaggerate evidential value and make p-hacking harder to detect, so lower power and reporting bias may impede detection from Abstracts even though Abstract p-values are more likely to concern primary hypotheses. Finding evidence in both Results and Abstracts is what the paper takes to support its conclusion that p-hacking is "rife".

## Study 2: p-values from twelve published meta-analyses

P-values were obtained for the primary studies used in published meta-analyses by evolutionary biologists studying sexual selection, giving 12 datasets (Table 3; meta-analyses with ten or fewer significant p-values are not shown). The paper notes that reporting the p-value for each effect size is not currently standard practice in meta-analyses and recommends it.

Evidential value. Pooled across the 12 datasets, the proportion in the upper evidential-value bin (0.025 to 0.05) was 0.202 (95% CI 0.179 to 0.228), p < 0.001. Nine of the 12 p-curves individually showed significant evidential value; the three that did not had the three lowest sample sizes, which the paper attributes to low power.

P-hacking. Sixteen p-values reported as p < 0.05 were found to be actually larger; the paper calls these misreported and runs the p-hacking test both with and without them. With misreported p-values included, 7 of 12 p-curves had more p-values in the upper bin than the lower, and the excess was significant in one dataset, Jiang et al. (2013) on assortative mating, which was also the largest (7 in the lower bin against 17 in the upper, p = 0.032; excluding misreported p-values, 7 against 11, p = 0.240). Pooled across the 12 datasets, the proportion in the upper bin was 0.615 (lower CI 0.513), p = 0.033, with misreported p-values included, and 0.489 (lower CI 0.375), p = 0.443, with them excluded. The paper acknowledges that including misreported p-values in the upper bin could be argued to bias the test towards detecting p-hacking, but holds that misreporting is part of p-hacking.

## What the paper claims

The paper claims two lines of evidence that p-hacking is widespread: the text-mining result across disciplines and the meta-analysis result on specific hypotheses. It reads both as showing evidential value for the claims researchers make, together with mean effect sizes that have probably been inflated by p-hacking. It says its results indicate that studies on questions important enough to warrant a meta-analysis "tend to be p-hacked".

On consequences, the paper's position is that the effect of p-hacking seems weak relative to the real effect sizes being measured, so p-hacking probably does not drastically alter scientific consensuses drawn from meta-analyses. For the one dataset with significant p-hacking, it argues the qualitative conclusion is unlikely to change because evidential value was strong and the 0.045 to 0.05 bin held only a small proportion of the significant p-values, although the mean effect size might have been inflated. Two general reasons are given for why meta-analyses might be robust: studies most susceptible to p-hacking are small ones, which receive less weight, and, at least in ecology and evolution, meta-analyses often use data that were secondary to the original paper's focus and so less likely to be p-hacked.

It also reads the strong right skew in every p-curve as reassuring against the concern that most published findings are false, while noting that text-mining pools many question types and cannot rule out that some fields or questions within a discipline have a flat p-curve.

Two ways of checking sensitivity are suggested: randomly remove the number of studies that make up the hump just below 0.05, or estimate effect sizes from p-curves using only significant p-values.

## What the paper concedes

- The sign test on the 0.025 threshold detects only severe p-hacking; the 0.005-wide bin test is more sensitive but its power falls with bin width, and right skew from true effects masks p-hacking.
- Text-mining combines many kinds of question, so it cannot show whether some fields or questions have zero average effect size; p-curves for well-defined questions are needed for that.
- Abstract p-values are subject to reporting bias that inflates apparent evidential value and hides p-hacking.
- Including misreported p-values biases the meta-analysis test towards detecting p-hacking; excluding them removes the evidence.
- Questions subjected to meta-analysis may not be a representative sample of research questions.
- P-curve methods are still in development, and real data are likely to violate assumptions of the simulations that have tested them.
- The choice of p < 0.05 rather than p = 0.05 as the upper cutoff rests on the authors' suspicion about reporting habits, not on a test.

## Recommendations

The paper argues that reducing p-hacking depends mainly on educating researchers, since many practices that lead to it are still regarded as acceptable; it supports this with a survey of psychologists (John et al., 2012) and an anonymous reviewer's report that some animal ethics boards encourage or mandate stopping a study once a significant result is obtained.

For researchers: label research as prespecified or exploratory; adhere to common analysis standards, measure only response variables known or predicted to matter, and use sufficient sample sizes; analyse data blind where possible; and weigh the quality of methods over the significance or novelty of results when assessing research.

For journals: require full reporting of analyses, including effect sizes whether small or large, p-values to three decimal places, sample sizes and the whole analysis process; encourage or provide platforms for prespecification; and encourage or provide platforms for open access to raw data, which the paper says does not prevent p-hacking but makes researchers accountable for marginal results and allows reanalysis.
