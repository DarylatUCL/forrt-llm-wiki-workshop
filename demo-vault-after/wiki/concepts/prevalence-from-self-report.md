# How prevalent are questionable research practices, according to self-report surveys?

A self-report survey asks researchers directly whether they have used a given practice, rather than inferring its use from the statistical properties of published results. This is a different measurement approach from the p-curve and text-mining methods covered on [[p-curve-as-evidence]] and [[text-mined-p-values]]: those ask what a distribution of published p-values implies about undisclosed practice; a self-report survey asks researchers to state their own practice, and separately, their estimate of how common the practice is among colleagues. The wiki holds three sources built on this method, in different research populations and with different survey instruments.

## What has been surveyed

Fraser and colleagues surveyed 807 ecology and evolutionary biology researchers (494 ecologists, 313 evolutionary biologists) about ten questionable research practices, asking, for each, how often the researcher had personally used it, how often they believed it should be used, and what percentage of colleagues in their field they believed had used it at least once [[fraser-2018]]. The ten practices are listed in full on [[fraser-2018]]; three of them, cherry picking, p hacking and HARKing, are named and defined in terms that match the practices discussed on [[what-p-hacking-is]] [[fraser-2018]].

Agnoli and colleagues surveyed 277 Italian research psychologists (208 of whom completed the survey), directly replicating John et al.'s (2012) US survey of ten questionable research practices, translated into Italian [[agnoli-2017]]. For each practice they asked a binary self-admission question (had the respondent personally ever used it, yes or no), a defensibility judgement (no, possibly, or yes), and two further questions about the respondent's beliefs about colleagues' use, described below [[agnoli-2017]]. The ten practices are listed in full on [[agnoli-2017]]; they are John et al.'s (2012) own item wordings, not Agnoli and colleagues' own construction [[agnoli-2017]].

The two surveys are not the same instrument. Fraser and colleagues measure frequency of personal use on a five-point scale (never to almost always) and collapse it to "at least once" for headline reporting; Agnoli and colleagues ask a single yes/no self-admission question with no frequency scale [[fraser-2018]] [[agnoli-2017]]. Both report use "at least once", though the underlying question format differs [[fraser-2018]] [[agnoli-2017]].

Gopalakrishna and colleagues surveyed academic researchers across all fields and ranks in the Netherlands; 6,813 completed the survey [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Their instrument differs from both of the others. Its 11 practices, adapted from a survey of research integrity conference participants, are mostly not the p-hacking practices the other two surveys ask about: they include insufficient supervision of junior co-workers, inadequate notes of the research process, selective citation, insufficient reporting of study flaws, and not submitting valid negative studies for publication [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Each is scored from 1 (never) to 7 (always) with a "not applicable" option, for the last three years only; a practice counts as prevalent for a respondent who scores it 5, 6 or 7, and respondents who answered "not applicable" are left out of the denominator [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The survey also asks about fabrication and falsification using the randomised response technique, which the paper describes as eliciting more honest answers to sensitive questions [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Its prevalence figures therefore measure frequent use within a three-year window, not use at least once ever.

## Headline rates

Across the full sample, 64% of Fraser and colleagues' researchers reported having, at least once, failed to report results because they were not statistically significant (cherry picking); 42% reported having collected more data after inspecting whether results were significant (a form of p hacking); and 51% reported having presented an unexpected finding as though it had been hypothesised from the start (HARKing) [[fraser-2018]]. These are combined figures across the ecology and evolution sub-samples; the by-discipline breakdown, with 95% confidence intervals, covers six practices and is given in full on [[fraser-2018]], alongside the two published psychology surveys the paper compares itself against [[fraser-2018]]. The three-population comparison in that table is Fraser and colleagues' own; the wiki does not extend it to any other source.

Agnoli and colleagues report a mean self-admission rate of 27.3% across their ten QRPs, with 88% of those who finished the survey admitting at least one of the ten [[agnoli-2017]]. The paper's own point of comparison is a US survey (John et al., 2012) and, in its Discussion, a German survey (Fiedler and Schwarz, 2016); neither is a paper the wiki has ingested, so both are named here as the paper's own comparators rather than as findings the wiki has independently verified, and their figures are not restated here [[agnoli-2017]]. The by-practice self-admission rates, with the US figures the paper reports alongside them, are given in full on [[agnoli-2017]].

The wiki does not place Fraser and colleagues' headline rates and Agnoli and colleagues' mean self-admission rate side by side. The two surveys sample different populations (ecology and evolutionary biology researchers against Italian research psychologists), use different item sets beyond the handful that name the same practice, and report prevalence in different units (a whole-sample percentage per named practice against a ten-item mean); Fraser and colleagues compare their by-discipline rates with Agnoli and colleagues' per-practice rates for the practices the two surveys share [[fraser-2018]], and the wiki does not extend that comparison to these headline figures. Combining the two into a ranking or a single figure would be exactly the kind of comparison section 3 of the schema rules out unless the qualifications are stated in the same place and the comparison is marked as loose, and the wiki has no reason drawn from either paper to make that comparison here.

Gopalakrishna and colleagues report that 51.3% (95% CI 50.1 to 52.5) of their Dutch respondents scored at least one of the 11 practices 5, 6 or 7 for the last three years ("any frequent QRP") [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Single practices ranged from 0.6% (improper referencing of sources) to 17.5% (not submitting or resubmitting valid negative studies), each among respondents who deemed the practice applicable [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Any frequent QRP was highest in life and medical sciences (55.3%) and lowest in arts and humanities (42.1%), the field with the most "not applicable" answers [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. For fabrication and falsification in the last three years, the randomised-response estimates are 4.3% (2.9 to 5.7) and 4.2% (2.8 to 5.6), and 8.3% (6.2 to 10.3) for either [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. These figures are not set beside Fraser and colleagues' or Agnoli and colleagues' rates: the items, window, threshold and denominator all differ. The paper itself says 51.3% suggests QRPs may be more prevalent than previously reported, and in the same passage says the figure may reflect its cut-off and that differing cut-offs, answer scales and QRP sets make results between surveys not directly comparable [[gopalakrishna-2022]].

## Frequency of use

Reporting having used a practice "at least once" is not the same as using it often. Fraser and colleagues report that it was extremely rare for researchers to describe their own use of any of the ten practices as "frequently" or "almost always"; most reported use fell at "once" or "occasionally," and many researchers reported never having used a given practice [[fraser-2018]]. Age and career stage were weak predictors of how often a researcher used these practices (Kendall's Tau of 0.05 and 0.04 respectively), while how often a researcher believed a practice should be used correlated considerably with how often they used it (Kendall's Tau = 0.6) [[fraser-2018]]. Agnoli and colleagues did not ask a frequency question; their self-admission item is binary, so this page has no equivalent frequency data from that survey [[agnoli-2017]].

Gopalakrishna and colleagues' headline measure is itself a frequency threshold: a score of 5, 6 or 7 on a seven-point scale labelled only at "never" and "always" [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The paper concedes that a wider definition of "frequent" would have given higher estimates [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Fraser and colleagues' finding that "frequently" or "almost always" was extremely rare and Gopalakrishna and colleagues' finding that half of respondents scored at least one practice 5 or above do not measure the same thing, since the practice sets, scales and scale labels differ [[fraser-2018]] [[gopalakrishna-2022]].

## Defensibility judgements

Agnoli and colleagues, following John et al.'s (2012) design, also asked whether each practice was defensible (no, possibly, or yes) [[agnoli-2017]]. For the eight QRPs with enough admissions to analyse, Italian respondents who had admitted using the practice most often answered "possibly" rather than "yes" (fewer than 20% answered "no"), and Italian respondents who had not admitted using it most often answered "no"; the exception on both counts was collecting more data after checking significance, where 55% of admitting respondents called the practice defensible and only 28% of non-admitting respondents called it indefensible, even though a majority of the whole sample had used it, which the paper reads as evidence that this practice's statistical consequences are not well understood in this sample [[agnoli-2017]]. Fraser and colleagues did not ask a defensibility question in this form; their nearest item asks how often a practice should be used, on a four-point frequency scale, which is a different construct from a categorical defensibility judgement, so the two are not compared here [[fraser-2018]] [[agnoli-2017]].

## Self-report against estimated colleague use

Fraser and colleagues' survey asked researchers to estimate colleague prevalence as well as to report their own use. They found the two closely related in general, but noted five practices, out of the ten, where the estimated prevalence among colleagues was substantially higher than self-reported personal use, and read that gap as marking those five as the least socially acceptable of the set; where the two measures were close, for four other practices, they read the practice as more socially acceptable and, on that account, potentially harder to shift because researchers may not recognise it as problematic [[fraser-2018]]. The paper offers this discrepancy, self-report below colleague estimate, as a reason to expect self-report to underestimate the true prevalence of the least acceptable practices [[fraser-2018]].

Agnoli and colleagues asked a similarly structured pair of questions (a prevalence estimate about colleagues, and an admission estimate: what percentage of users would admit it), and also found their derived and estimated prevalence figures well above the self-admission rate [[agnoli-2017]]. They draw a different conclusion from the gap than Fraser and colleagues do: rather than reading it as a signal of a practice's social acceptability, Agnoli and colleagues argue, citing their respondents' own comments about the difficulty of estimating other researchers' behaviour, that the prevalence estimate and the derived estimate are not valid measures of actual prevalence at all, and that self-admission, though probably an underestimate, is the more defensible figure [[agnoli-2017]]. The two readings conflict on what the gap can show: Fraser and colleagues take a large gap to suggest that self-report underestimates prevalence [[fraser-2018]], while Agnoli and colleagues hold that estimates of other researchers' behaviour are of questionable validity as estimates of behaviour [[agnoli-2017]]. Neither paper addresses the other's reading directly, and the wiki records both without resolving them.

## Doubts about scientific integrity

Fraser and colleagues' survey asked researchers how often they had doubts about the scientific integrity, separately for questionable research practices and for scientific misconduct, of five groups: researchers at other institutions, research at their own institution, graduate-student research at their institution, senior colleagues or collaborators, and their own research. The full figures, with 95% confidence intervals, are on [[fraser-2018]]. Concern about integrity at the researcher's own institution was roughly equal to concern about other institutions, and there was no notable difference in concern about graduate students against senior colleagues; participants expressed the least concern about their own integrity, though 44.6% reported having had such doubts about their own use of QRPs once or twice and a further 3.2% often (the paper's summary sentence gives 44.6% as the share with doubts; its Table 3 splits the figure as here) [[fraser-2018]].

Agnoli and colleagues asked a similarly themed but differently structured question: how often, on a four-point scale (never / once or twice / occasionally / often), respondents doubted the integrity of themselves, their collaborators, graduate students at their institution, researchers at their institution, and researchers at other institutions [[agnoli-2017]]. About 35% of Italian respondents reported doubting their own integrity on at least one occasion, and about 51% reported occasional or frequent doubts about researchers at other institutions, with strong confidence expressed in collaborators and in the respondent's own work [[agnoli-2017]]. Because the two surveys use different institutional categories and different response scales, their figures are not placed side by side here [[fraser-2018]] [[agnoli-2017]].

## Temptation to use QRPs

Agnoli and colleagues asked, uniquely among the sources on this page, whether the respondent had personally been tempted, in the past year, to use a QRP to improve their chances of publication or career advancement: of 202 respondents, 53% said never, 37% once or twice, 7% occasionally, and 3% often [[agnoli-2017]].

## Career stage and self-admission

Agnoli and colleagues found self-admission rising monotonically with career stage, from 25.7% among post-docs and PhD students to 28.4% among full professors, though the differences between adjacent career stages are small, and the paper attributes this partly to more senior researchers having had more opportunities to use a QRP [[agnoli-2017]]. Fraser and colleagues report a separate finding that touches career stage: a weak correlation (Kendall's Tau = 0.04) between career stage and how often, not whether, a researcher used a QRP [[fraser-2018]]. The two findings use different measures of career stage, different QRP outcomes (ever-used group means against a within-person frequency correlation), and different statistical tests, so the wiki records them as separate rather than as agreeing or disagreeing. Gopalakrishna and colleagues find a different direction on their own measure: PhD candidates and junior researchers had higher odds of any frequent QRP in the last three years than postdocs and assistant professors (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.32) [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. This and Agnoli and colleagues' finding measure different outcomes (frequent use in a fixed window against use ever) over different practice sets; the disagreement is set out on [[factors-associated-with-qrp-use]].

## What the surveys cannot show

Fraser and colleagues state that their sample, drawn from corresponding authors in high-impact-factor journals, likely under-represents graduate students (6% of respondents) and should be read as reflecting mainly postdoctoral, mid-career and senior researchers rather than the discipline as a whole [[fraser-2018]]. They expect a self-selection bias among respondents and reason that, if more methodologically confident researchers were more likely to respond, the effect would most likely be an underestimate of QRP rates in the wider community rather than an overestimate [[fraser-2018]]. To protect anonymity the survey did not record country of origin, so any cross-country variation of the kind the two psychology surveys suggest could not be examined [[fraser-2018]]. The paper also flags two of its own items as interpretively unclear from the response categories alone: whether an unstated but implied hypothesis counts as HARKing, and whether excluding data after a significance check that was in fact prompted by a discovered model error should count as the same practice as excluding data because of the check itself [[fraser-2018]].

Agnoli and colleagues allow that the small but significant US/Italy differences on three of the ten QRPs could reflect sampling bias in either country's voluntary-response survey, or a failure of the translated items to be fully measurement-invariant across languages [[agnoli-2017]]. They judge the self-admission rate likely to be an underestimate of true prevalence, though they cite John et al.'s finding that an incentive-for-honesty manipulation raised self-admission only modestly as evidence the underestimate is probably not large overall (the figure is on [[agnoli-2017]]) [[agnoli-2017]]. Too few respondents in either country admitted QRPs 9 and 10 for a statistical comparison of defensibility judgements to be run [[agnoli-2017]]. The paper also offers one possible reason for the lower overall self-admission rates in Italy and Germany, namely that those surveys took place later, after the US survey was published and discussion of the reproducibility crisis had begun, while adding that the Italian survey was run in 2014, only two years after that publication [[agnoli-2017]].

Gopalakrishna and colleagues could calculate a response rate only for the eight of 22 institutions that supplied email addresses (21.2%), and say they cannot assess the sample's representativeness, even on background characteristics, because no national figures match their eligibility criteria [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. They concede that recoding "not applicable" to "never" in their mean-score analyses may underestimate intentional QRPs, and that their practice set may not capture what counts as a QRP in the arts and humanities [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. They cannot tell whether the higher fabrication and falsification estimate in life and medical sciences (10.4%) reflects more misconduct or more willingness to report it [[gopalakrishna-2022]].
