# Can p-hacking be detected from the distribution of published p-values?

A p-curve is the distribution of p-values reported for a set of studies [[head-2015]]. The idea examined here is that the shape of the curve within the significant range carries information about how the p-values were produced, and that a surplus just below 0.05 is a signature of p-hacking [[head-2015]]. One source applies that logic to large text-mined and meta-analytic datasets [[head-2015]]; a second tests it by simulation and disputes that it can measure the extent of p-hacking or of evidential value [[bishop-2016]]; a third reanalyses the text-mined data and reports that the surplus disappears under other defensible choices of bins [[hartgerink-2017]]; a fourth simulates p-hacking in observational research and reports that null effects with omitted-variable bias produce the right skew that is read as evidence of a true effect, and no left skew at all [[bruns-2016]].

## The logic of the test

When the true effect is zero, p-values are uniformly distributed, so the expected p-curve is flat; when the true effect is non-zero, the expected curve is right skewed, and more so the larger the effect [[head-2015]]. A right skew among significant p-values is read as "evidential value", meaning the published evidence suggests a non-zero effect [[head-2015]]. Bishop and Thompson accept this much, adding that the degree of right skew grows with sample size because larger studies have more power [[bishop-2016]].

A drop in p-values just above 0.05 is consistent with either selective publication or p-hacking and does not tell the two apart [[head-2015]]. What is claimed to distinguish p-hacking is an overabundance of p-values just below 0.05: with no true effect, p-hacking turns a flat curve into a left-skewed one, and with a true effect it leaves the right skew but adds a bump in the tail just below 0.05 [[head-2015]]. The exact shape depends on both the true effect and the intensity of p-hacking [[head-2015]]. Bishop and Thompson, citing Simonsohn and colleagues, describe the left skew as arising under certain circumstances, when researchers adopt extreme methods such as adding covariates or removing subjects to push nearly significant results below .05 [[bishop-2016]].

Hartgerink puts the same logic as a distinction between sufficient and necessary conditions. A systematic bump below .05, one not due to sampling error, is sufficient for the presence of specific forms of p-hacking, such as optional stopping when the null hypothesis is true, and Head et al. are said to argue correctly that frequent left-skew p-hacking could produce such a bump in an aggregate distribution [[hartgerink-2017]]. It is not necessary: optional stopping under a true effect, or reporting only the smallest of several p-values, can occur without a bump, and some forms of p-hacking produce right skew instead, so finding no bump does not exclude large-scale p-hacking [[hartgerink-2017]].

Bruns and Ioannidis state the same reading, attributing it to Simonsohn and colleagues: true effects give right skew, null effects give a uniform distribution, and null effects with p-hacking give left skew or a peak just below 0.05, on the intuition that p-hacked studies reach p-values that are just significant while values near zero are hard to obtain by p-hacking [[bruns-2016]]. They accept that the two determinants of a p-curve are the effect size and the sample size, but reply that the estimated effect size can differ from zero because of a bias rather than a true effect, which is the basis of the objection set out below [[bruns-2016]].

## The assumptions the test rests on

Bishop and Thompson state, attributing the conditions to Simonsohn and colleagues, that valid inference from a p-curve requires the selected p-values to test the hypothesis of interest, to be statistically independent of each other, and to be uniformly distributed under the null [[bishop-2016]]. They report that the uniformity condition has been questioned, for discrete outcomes and for small-sample t-tests with unequal variances, and that this raises the question of how robust p-curve analysis is when the underlying data violate its assumptions [[bishop-2016]].

## Tests that have been used

Evidential value has been tested with a two-tailed sign test comparing the number of p-values in the bin 0 to 0.025 against the bin 0.025 to 0.05, a modification of a test proposed by Simonsohn and colleagues [[head-2015]]. That test is described as detecting only severe p-hacking, because a strong true effect masks the left skew p-hacking adds [[head-2015]].

The more sensitive test used by Head et al. is a one-tailed sign test asking whether the bin 0.045 < p < 0.05 holds more p-values than the adjacent bin 0.04 < p < 0.045, with the proportion in the upper bin and a Clopper-Pearson 95% interval as the measure of strength [[head-2015]]. The bin width of 0.005 trades sensitivity against sample size, and the upper cutoff excludes p = 0.05 because the authors suspect many researchers do not treat it as significant [[head-2015]]. Results across disciplines or datasets are pooled with a binomial generalised linear model that tests whether the proportion in the upper bin differs from 0.5 [[head-2015]].

Hartgerink identifies this two-bin comparison as the Caliper test used earlier in publication bias research, and notes a second exclusion besides p = 0.05: p = 0.045 falls into neither bin, having been left out to keep the bins symmetrical [[hartgerink-2017]].

## Findings reported by the sources

Applied to p-values text-mined from all open-access papers in PubMed, with bin counts averaged over 1,000 bootstraps of one p-value per paper section and disciplines with fewer than 50 significant p-values excluded, the pooled proportion of p-values in the 0.045 to 0.05 bin was 0.546 (one-tailed lower CI 0.536), p < 0.001, across 14 disciplines for Results sections, and 0.537 (lower CI 0.518), p < 0.001, across 10 disciplines for Abstracts [[head-2015]]. Every discipline also showed significant evidential value [[head-2015]]. Individually, the excess in the upper bin was significant for Results-section p-values in Medical and health sciences (1,477 against 1,785, p < 0.001), Biological sciences (350 against 423, p = 0.005), Multidisciplinary (638 against 750, p = 0.001) and Psychology and cognitive sciences (29 against 50, p = 0.012), and for Abstract p-values only in Multidisciplinary and Information and computing sciences [[head-2015]]. Head et al. conclude from this that p-hacking is widespread [[head-2015]].

Applied to p-values from the primary studies of 12 published sexual-selection meta-analyses, the pooled proportion in the upper bin was 0.615 (lower CI 0.513), p = 0.033, when 16 p-values misreported as p < 0.05 were included, and 0.489 (lower CI 0.375), p = 0.443, when they were excluded; only one dataset, the largest, was individually significant [[head-2015]].

Whether the text-mined p-values were suitable inputs for these tests is disputed; see [[text-mined-p-values]].

## Is the bump robust to how the bins are chosen?

Hartgerink reanalysed the same subset of Head et al.'s text-mined data, the exactly reported p-values below .05, without the two exclusions [[hartgerink-2017]]. Plotted at a binwidth of .00125, the distribution shows systematically more p-values at .01, .02, .03, .04 and .05 than at neighbouring three-decimal values, which Hartgerink reads as a tendency to report p-values to two decimal places, and no clear bump below .05, since whether one appears depends on which bins are compared [[hartgerink-2017]]. Simply including p = .045 leaves 20,114 p-values in .04 < p <= .045 against 18,132 in .045 < p < .05, the opposite direction to a bump; the paper does not say which paper sections these counts cover [[hartgerink-2017]].

Three tests are reported, each finding no bump. In all of them the proportion is the share of p-values falling in the bin nearer .05 [[hartgerink-2017]].

- Fisher's method on all p-values in .04 < p < .05, transformed to test for a surplus towards .05: chi-squared(76,492) = 70,328.86, p > .999 [[hartgerink-2017]].
- A Caliper test on bins built around the two-decimal values, .03875 < p <= .04 against .04875 < p <= .05, keeping p = .045 and p = .05, with a Bayesian version under an ultrawide prior: for Results sections 26,047 against 18,664, proportion .417, p > .999, BF10 < .001; for Abstracts 4,597 against 2,565, proportion .358, p > .999, BF10 < .001. Widening the bins to .005 and .01 gave proportions of .441 and .448 for Results and .403 and .423 for Abstracts, all with p > .999 and BF10 < .001, and no discipline was individually significant in either section [[hartgerink-2017]].
- Head et al.'s original bins with the second decimal included, .04 <= p < .045 against .045 < p <= .05: proportion .431, p > .999, BF10 < .001; the section is not stated [[hartgerink-2017]].

Hartgerink's stated reasons for keeping the two values are that p = .045 was dropped only for bin symmetry, that researchers treat p = .05 as significant more often than Head et al. supposed (citing Nuijten et al., 2015), and that strategic rounding down to p = .05 is itself p-hacking, so excluding p = .05 lowers the test's sensitivity [[hartgerink-2017]]. The conclusion drawn is that the evidence for a bump is not robust to minor, justifiable changes in the analysis; Head et al.'s conclusion might still be correct, but on this reading the data do not show it undisputably [[hartgerink-2017]]. Whether one p-value per paper was sampled, as Head et al. did, is not stated in the reanalysis [[hartgerink-2017]].

## Does a right skew show a true effect in observational research?

Bruns and Ioannidis examine the other half of the logic, the reading of right skew as evidence of a true effect, for research without randomisation [[bruns-2016]]. Their argument is that in regression on observational data the main way to obtain a significant estimate is to vary the set of adjusting variables, and that leaving out a variable which affects the outcome and is correlated with the variable of interest produces omitted-variable bias [[bruns-2016]]. Such a bias makes the estimate biased and inconsistent, so the p-value approaches zero as the sample grows whether or not a true effect exists; this is a systematic, asymptotic bias, unlike p-hacking under randomisation, which relies on chance while estimation stays unbiased [[bruns-2016]]. A null effect with omitted-variable bias therefore produces the same right skew as a true effect [[bruns-2016]].

In their Monte Carlo simulations the true effect is zero, a random omitted-variable bias is drawn in each of 500,000 iterations, and only the significant estimates are kept [[bruns-2016]]. The bias is scaled so that the maximum expected correlation between outcome and predictor is 0.01, 0.05 or 0.1, which the paper places between one tenth of a small effect and a small effect on Cohen's conventions, and the sample size is uniform between 50 and a maximum of 100, 1,000, 10,000 or 100,000 [[bruns-2016]]. The p-curves become right-skewed once the sample is large enough, and none is left-skewed or peaks just below 0.05 [[bruns-2016]]. The share of iterations reaching p < 0.05, read from the figure legends rather than the text, rises from 5.04% to 23.88% across the four sample-size settings at the smallest bias and from 8.11% to 89.52% at the largest; the right skew is pronounced only at the largest sample size for the smallest bias, and present at every sample size for the largest [[bruns-2016]]. The paper calls this simulation conservative because every variable is resampled in each iteration rather than searching one dataset for a significant specification [[bruns-2016]].

An empirical illustration uses a cross-country growth dataset with growth of real GDP per capita from 1960 to 1996 as the outcome, malaria prevalence in 1966 as the effect of interest, and 15 adjusting variables [[bruns-2016]]. A new growth variable is constructed so that the malaria effect is exactly zero while the rest of the data's structure is retained (the original estimate was -0.00764, p = 0.224, and the constructed and original growth variables correlate at 0.987) [[bruns-2016]]. With 6 of the 15 adjusters included in each model there are 5,005 models; across 100 random samples of between 50 and 99 countries, 23.4% of the 500,500 estimates are negative and significant at p < 0.05 and 62.6% negative and not significant [[bruns-2016]]. P-hacking is modelled as browsing the models on a sample until a negative significant estimate appears, then drawing a new sample, until 100,000 such estimates are collected; the resulting p-curve is right-skewed with no peak below 0.05, and a rerun on the full 99 countries every time gives the same shape [[bruns-2016]].

The conclusion drawn is that in observational research a p-curve may identify neither true effects nor p-hacking, and that the distortion is proportional to the bias: with small samples a bias at the 0.1 level suffices, while with large samples a bias at the 0.01 level distorts p-values, in the paper's words, beyond repair [[bruns-2016]]. The paper adds that the same applies to nominally experimental studies whose randomisation has been subverted, and that other biases which make estimation inconsistent (simultaneity, misspecified functional form, measurement error) may produce right skew in the same way, though it simulates only omitted-variable bias [[bruns-2016]].

## What simulation shows about ghost variables

Bishop and Thompson simulated one form of p-hacking, the use of ghost variables, in which a study measures several dependent variables and reports only those with p < .05 [[bishop-2016]]. Each of 100,000 runs simulated a two-group study with 3 or 8 dependent variables, 20 or 200 subjects per group, and inter-correlations between the variables of 0, .5 or .8; one significant p-value was selected at random per study and studies with none were dropped [[bishop-2016]].

With no true effect, ghost p-hacking produced a flat p-curve when the variables were uncorrelated, and a left skew when they were correlated, with the slope growing with the correlation [[bishop-2016]]. The false positive rate in these simulations was around 40% with uncorrelated variables and around 12% at r = .8, and rose with the number of variables [[bishop-2016]]. Bishop and Thompson explain the skew as an artefact of selecting one p-value per study from correlated p-values: when a run's p-values sit just above .05, only those near the boundary are eligible, whereas uncorrelated p-values below .05 are all equally likely to be chosen [[bishop-2016]]. With a true effect of d = .3 on one variable, the expected right skew appeared, its extent depending on power and little affected by the number of variables [[bishop-2016]].

Two consequences are drawn. First, ghost p-hacking with uncorrelated or weakly correlated variables leaves no bump below .05 at all, so its absence does not indicate an absence of p-hacking [[bishop-2016]]. Second, when power is low and variables are highly correlated, mixing in p-hacked studies can cancel the right skew that true effects produce, so that a set of studies containing real effects need not show a right-skewed p-curve [[bishop-2016]].

## Power of the near-bin versus far-bin test

Bishop and Thompson estimated the power of the test that compares the .04 to .045 bin with the .045 to .05 bin in the extreme case most favourable to detection: no true effects, ghost p-hacking in every study, and eight variables inter-correlated at .8 [[bishop-2016]]. Even there the left skew was slight; around 1,200 p-values between .04 and .05 were needed for 80% power, and since only 4% of simulated studies had a p-value in that range, that corresponds to around 30,000 studies [[bishop-2016]]. They conclude that the near-bin versus far-bin test gives a conservative estimate of p-hacking and that the bin counts depend on five things that are unknown in real data: the number of studies, the proportion p-hacked, the number of variables, the sample size, and the correlation between variables [[bishop-2016]].

## Where the sources disagree

The sources agree that the tests have limited sensitivity. Head et al. say the broad sign test (0 to .025 against .025 to .05) and Simonsohn et al.'s tests detect severe but not modest p-hacking, especially when true effects are strong, and introduce the near-bin comparison as the more sensitive alternative [[head-2015]]; Bishop and Thompson say the near-bin test misses ghost p-hacking altogether when variables are uncorrelated and lacks power when they are correlated [[bishop-2016]].

They disagree about what a detected signal, or a right skew, licenses. Head et al. read a significant excess in the near bin as evidence that p-hacking is widespread, and read strong right skew in every discipline as evidence that researchers predominantly study real effects [[head-2015]]. Bishop and Thompson argue that the binomial test cannot quantify the amount of p-hacking in any p-curve, text-mined or not, and that the degree of right skew cannot quantify evidential value in heterogeneous data, because both depend on parameters that are not known: the proportion of p-hacked studies, sample sizes, and the number and correlation of dependent variables [[bishop-2016]]. They also note that the bump Head et al. observed could have causes other than the p-hacking mechanism they simulated, including the misreporting of p-values that Head et al. themselves found in their meta-analysis data [[bishop-2016]]. Their stated conclusion is that estimating the extent of p-hacking and evidential value from a p-curve is not feasible without considerable control over the type of data entered, although a p-curve can still indicate that something is problematic [[bishop-2016]].

Hartgerink's disagreement is with Head et al. over the data themselves rather than over what a signal licenses. On the reanalysis, the surplus in the near bin is a product of excluding p = .045 and p = .05 and of comparing bins that ignore two-decimal reporting; under the alternatives described above there is no surplus at all [[hartgerink-2017]]. Head et al. state their reason for excluding p = 0.05, the suspicion that many researchers do not treat it as significant [[head-2015]]; Hartgerink calls that assumption potentially invalid, and describes the exclusion of p = .045 as a by-product of needing symmetric bins [[hartgerink-2017]]. The sources contain no reply to the reanalysis.

On the wider question Hartgerink sides with neither reading outright. The paper accepts, with Head et al., that a bump would be sufficient evidence of certain forms of p-hacking [[hartgerink-2017]] [[head-2015]], and agrees with Bishop and Thompson that its absence does not show p-hacking to be absent [[hartgerink-2017]] [[bishop-2016]]. It adds two reasons of its own why aggregate p-curves cannot prove absence: an ecological fallacy, since individual papers may show a bump the aggregate hides, and right-skew forms of p-hacking that the near-bin test cannot see; its recommendation is that detection be attempted at the paper level after scrutiny of which results are included [[hartgerink-2017]].

Bruns and Ioannidis open a disagreement with Head et al. over the reading of right skew rather than of the bump. Head et al. read the strong right skew in every discipline as reassuring against the concern that most published findings are false, while conceding that text-mining pools many question types [[head-2015]]. Bruns and Ioannidis reply that Head et al.'s text-mined p-values come from both experimental and observational designs, that the conclusion rests on the assumption that right skew indicates true effects, and that this assumption may be false for the observational part, since a null effect with omitted-variable bias produces the same skew [[bruns-2016]]. They add that even for experimental designs a right-skewed p-curve implies only that some of the studies analyse true effects, citing a preprint by Bishop and Thompson (2015) as illustrating right skew when only a minority of studies test true effects (the figure is on [[bruns-2016]]), and they apply the same objection to a mixture-model estimate of the false discovery rate in the medical literature by Jager and Leek (2014), which on their account could be anything, even 100% [[bruns-2016]]. Their stated view is that little can be learned from such studies beyond an indication that p-curves may be right-skewed in some disciplines, with the source of the skew unexplained [[bruns-2016]]. The sources contain no reply from Head et al.

On this point Bruns and Ioannidis stand with Bishop and Thompson, who also hold that the degree of right skew cannot quantify evidential value in heterogeneous data [[bishop-2016]], but the two reach that position by different routes: Bishop and Thompson because the skew depends on power and can be cancelled by p-hacked correlated variables at low power [[bishop-2016]], Bruns and Ioannidis because a systematic bias generates skew without any true effect [[bruns-2016]]. On the bump, Bruns and Ioannidis find no left skew or peak in any simulated or illustrated p-curve [[bruns-2016]], which is the same conclusion Bishop and Thompson reach for ghost variables with uncorrelated variables [[bishop-2016]] and Hartgerink states as the bump not being a necessary condition [[hartgerink-2017]], each for a different mechanism of p-hacking. Bruns and Ioannidis concede one case in which right skew is read correctly: when omitted-variable bias is used to exaggerate a true effect rather than to make a null effect significant, so that a right-skewed curve is consistent with a null effect with p-hacking, a true effect, or an exaggerated true effect [[bruns-2016]].

The disagreement turns on three things. First, whether the p-curve tests are read as detectors (which Head et al., Bishop and Thompson and Hartgerink accept with reservations about sensitivity, and which Bruns and Ioannidis deny for observational research, where on their account p-curves may identify neither true effects nor p-hacking [[bruns-2016]]) or as measures of extent (which Head et al. use them as and Bishop and Thompson reject). Second, whether the text-mined data show a bump at all once the bins and exclusions are varied, which Head et al.'s analysis finds and Hartgerink's reanalysis does not. Third, whether a right skew licenses the inference to true effects, which Head et al. draw and Bruns and Ioannidis deny for observational research and treat as weak for experimental research. The wiki records all three as unresolved.

## Limits acknowledged by the sources

Head et al. state that their test is insensitive to modest p-hacking when true effects are strong, that narrower bins would be more sensitive but cost power, and that pooling many kinds of question in text-mined data cannot reveal whether some fields or questions have a flat p-curve [[head-2015]]. They also note that Abstract p-values are subject to reporting bias that inflates apparent evidential value and hides p-hacking, and that counting misreported p-values in the upper bin could be argued to bias the test towards finding p-hacking [[head-2015]].

Bishop and Thompson simulate only ghost variables, with a true effect on a single variable, and concede that the bump Head et al. observed could have arisen in other ways [[bishop-2016]]. Their power analysis covers one extreme parameter setting [[bishop-2016]]. They allow that p-curve analysis retains a place where a set of p-values from studies testing one hypothesis meets the conditions they attribute to Simonsohn and colleagues [[bishop-2016]].

Hartgerink concedes that the bins just below .04 and .05 are not adjacent, which may reduce sensitivity to a bump, and that restricting the data to exactly reported p-values may distort the distribution, since a p-value of .047 may be more likely to be reported as p < .05 than one of .037 as p < .04; the paper estimates the size of this rounding asymmetry from a figure in Krawczyk (2015) (the estimate is on [[hartgerink-2017]]), which could under-represent p-values near .05 [[hartgerink-2017]]. The explanation of two-decimal reporting by publication-manual standards is labelled post hoc [[hartgerink-2017]]. The paper repeats that no evidence of a bump is not evidence of no p-hacking [[hartgerink-2017]].

Bruns and Ioannidis concede that the omission of confounders is not necessarily p-hacking, since it may stem from unavailable data or from not knowing what to adjust for, and that experts rarely agree on the adjustment set [[bruns-2016]]. They allow that Simonsohn and colleagues introduced the p-curve primarily for experimental research, where right skew may be a sign of true effects [[bruns-2016]]. Their simulation covers omitted-variable bias only, the null effect in their illustration is constructed rather than established, and they note that the empirical calibration of p-values they suggest as a remedy is not always feasible [[bruns-2016]].
