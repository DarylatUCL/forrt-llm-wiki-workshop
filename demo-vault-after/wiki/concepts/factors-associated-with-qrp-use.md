# What is associated with researchers' use of questionable research practices?

Several sources argue that questionable research practices are driven by the incentive to publish (see [[what-p-hacking-is]]). This page collects what the self-report surveys actually measure alongside practice use: characteristics of the respondent, and scales for the conditions and norms they work under. All associations here are cross-sectional correlations within a single survey. For how common the practices are, see [[prevalence-from-self-report]].

## Explanatory factor scales in one national survey

Gopalakrishna and colleagues are the only source in the wiki that measures working conditions and norms with scales and relates them to practice use in the same respondents [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Their Dutch survey of 6,813 academic researchers entered 11 scales together in each model, adjusted for field, rank, gender, empirical research and institutional support [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The outcomes are their own constructs: "any frequent QRP" means a score of 5 to 7 on a 1 to 7 frequency scale for at least one of 11 practices over the last three years, and "any FF" means admitting fabrication or falsification under the randomised response technique [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Their 11 practices are mostly not the p-hacking practices named on [[what-p-hacking-is]]; they include inadequate supervision, poor note-taking, selective citation and not submitting valid negative studies [[gopalakrishna-2022]].

Per standard deviation increase, with 95% confidence intervals [[gopalakrishna-2022]]:

- Publication pressure: odds of any frequent QRP 1.22 (1.14 to 1.30); no significant association with any FF (OR 1.09, 0.75 to 1.59).
- Scientific norm subscription: odds of any frequent QRP 0.88 (0.83 to 0.93); any FF OR 0.79 (0.63 to 1.00).
- Peer norms and organisational justice: odds of any frequent QRP 0.91 each.
- Perceived likelihood of detection by reviewers: any FF OR 0.62 in the paper's text, 0.63 (0.44 to 0.88) in its Table 4; no association with the QRP outcomes.
- Work pressure, competitiveness and survival mentoring: small positive associations with the mean QRP score only; funding pressure and detection by collaborators: none.

The paper reads the publication pressure result as support for changing the "publish or perish" reward system, and the reviewer result as suggesting that reviewers may help prevent misconduct [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. These are the paper's causal readings of cross-sectional associations.

## Researcher characteristics

In the same survey, men had higher odds of any frequent QRP than women (women OR 0.77, 0.69 to 0.85), and respondents doing empirical research higher odds than those who did not; against life and medical sciences, every other field had lower odds, lowest in arts and humanities (OR 0.61, 0.50 to 0.74) [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. No background characteristic was significantly associated with fabrication or falsification [[gopalakrishna-2022]].

Fraser and colleagues, surveying ecology and evolutionary biology researchers, found age and career stage weakly related to how often a researcher used a practice (Kendall's Tau 0.05 and 0.04), and a researcher's view of how often a practice should be used correlating considerably with how often they used it (Tau 0.6) [[fraser-2018]]. Agnoli and colleagues found self-admission highest among Italian psychologists in the social, experimental and organisational divisions and lowest among developmental, educational and clinical researchers [[agnoli-2017]].

## Career stage: different directions on different measures

The surveys do not agree on the direction of the career-stage association, and they measure different things.

- Gopalakrishna and colleagues find PhD candidates and junior researchers at higher odds of any frequent QRP than postdocs and assistant professors (OR 1.16, 1.01 to 1.32), with no significant difference for associate and full professors; the outcome is frequent use within the last three years [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The paper relays an earlier suggestion that inexperience and poor supervision may lead junior researchers into QRPs unintentionally [[gopalakrishna-2022]].
- Agnoli and colleagues find self-admission of ever having used a practice rising slightly with seniority, from 25.7% among post-docs and PhD students to 28.4% among full professors, and attribute this partly to senior researchers having had more opportunity [[agnoli-2017]].
- Fraser and colleagues find only a weak correlation between career stage and frequency of use [[fraser-2018]].

The disagreement turns on the outcome (frequent use in a fixed window against use ever), on the practice sets, which overlap little, and on the populations. The wiki does not decide which pattern holds.
