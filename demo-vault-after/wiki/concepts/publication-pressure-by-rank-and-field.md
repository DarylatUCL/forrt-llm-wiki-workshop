# How does perceived publication pressure vary by academic rank and disciplinary field?

Perceived publication pressure is the subjective pressure a researcher feels from the sense that they have to publish, distinct from whether that pressure produces stress, which depends on the resources available to manage it [[haven-2019a]]. This page collects what two sources report about measuring that pressure directly with a scale: the revised Publication Pressure Questionnaire (PPQr) in an Amsterdam survey [[haven-2019a]], and a single publication pressure scale in a national Dutch survey [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The two instruments are not the same and their scores are not merged. For the practices publication pressure is said to incentivise, see [[what-p-hacking-is]]; this page does not measure those practices, only the pressure itself, and the two are not merged here.

## The instrument

The PPQr scores three subscales on a 5-point Likert scale: Publication Stress (the stress felt from having to publish), Publication Attitude (the researcher's attitude toward the current publication climate), and Publication Resources (factors, such as supportive colleagues or a sense of competence, that can help offset publication stress) [[haven-2019a]]. A higher score on every subscale means more stress, a more negative attitude, and fewer perceived resources respectively [[haven-2019a]]. The source is explicit that the PPQr measures publication pressure specifically, and that it cannot separate how much of a researcher's overall pressure comes from publishing as against other professional demands such as teaching [[haven-2019a]].

## Sample

A survey of all academic researchers, including PhD students, employed at least one day a week at one of four Amsterdam academic institutions; 7,548 were invited, 1,073 (14%) completed the PPQr [[haven-2019a]]. Respondents are classified by academic rank (PhD student, postdoc, assistant professor, associate professor, full professor, with postdocs and assistant professors grouped together and associate and full professors grouped together for the pairwise comparisons below) and by disciplinary field (biomedicine, natural sciences, social sciences, humanities) [[haven-2019a]].

## Overall levels

Across the full sample, researchers scored highest on Attitude (M = 3.59), indicating a substantial negative attitude toward the current publication climate; somewhat lower on Stress (M = 3.22); and lowest on Resources (M = 2.21), indicating a comparatively small perceived lack of resources [[haven-2019a]].

## Differences by academic rank

Postdocs and assistant professors report the most publication stress (M = 3.42), significantly more than both PhD students and associate and full professors, and, along with PhD students, a more negative attitude than associate and full professors, on Bonferroni-corrected, confounder-adjusted pairwise comparisons [[haven-2019a]]. PhD students report the greatest perceived lack of resources (M = 2.44), significantly more than every other rank group; postdocs and assistant professors in turn perceive fewer resources than associate and full professors [[haven-2019a]]. The source attributes the postdoc/assistant-professor stress peak to this group actively pursuing a tenured position under largely quantitative promotion criteria, and the comparatively lower PhD student stress to thesis defence not usually depending on publication count, while reading PhD students' resource shortfall as consistent with their relative inexperience in navigating the publication process [[haven-2019a]].

## Differences by disciplinary field

Field differences were significant but smaller than rank differences [[haven-2019a]]. Humanities researchers report more stress than biomedicine and natural sciences researchers, and social sciences researchers report more stress than biomedicine researchers; there were no significant field differences on Attitude [[haven-2019a]]. Natural sciences researchers perceive significantly more resources than either biomedicine or social sciences researchers, which the source attributes to the field's typical organisation into large collaborative teams; but PhD students within natural sciences perceive a shortfall of resources similar to PhD students in other fields, an effect-modification finding the source attributes tentatively to possibly insufficient mentoring specific to that group [[haven-2019a]].

## A national survey with a single scale

Gopalakrishna and colleagues' National Survey on Research Integrity measured publication pressure as one of 12 explanatory factor scales, scored 1 to 7, among 6,813 academic researchers across the Netherlands [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The scale is reported as a single score (Cronbach's alpha 0.80), not split into stress, attitude and resources, and the paper says it had been used before in highly similar samples without naming it in the extracted text [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Ranks are grouped into three (PhD candidates and junior researchers; postdocs and assistant professors; associate and full professors) and fields into four (life and medical sciences; social and behavioural sciences; natural and engineering sciences; arts and humanities) [[gopalakrishna-2022]].

The overall mean was 3.9 [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Postdocs and assistant professors scored highest (4.2), against 3.8 for PhD candidates and junior researchers and 3.7 for associate and full professors [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Arts and humanities scored highest among fields (4.1), against 3.8 to 4.0 elsewhere [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. These are descriptive means; the paper reports no significance tests for the rank and field differences [[gopalakrishna-2022]].

Unlike the Amsterdam survey, this one also asked respondents about questionable research practices. Each standard deviation increase in publication pressure was associated with 1.22 times the odds (95% CI 1.14 to 1.30) of scoring at least one of 11 practices 5 or above on a 1 to 7 frequency scale over the last three years, adjusted for background characteristics and the other scales; it was not significantly associated with fabrication or falsification [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. See [[factors-associated-with-qrp-use]].

## How the two surveys' rank and field patterns relate

Both surveys find the postdoc and assistant professor group at the top on publication pressure: on the PPQr Stress subscale in Amsterdam [[haven-2019a]], and on the single scale nationally [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. Both also place the humanities, or arts and humanities, high: more stress than biomedicine and natural sciences on the PPQr [[haven-2019a]], and the highest field mean on the national scale [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. This is a loose correspondence of direction only. The instruments differ (a three-subscale 5-point questionnaire against a single 7-point scale), the samples differ (four Amsterdam institutions against a national sample), rank and field groupings differ, and only the Amsterdam paper tests the differences for significance.

## What the sources concede

The 14% completion rate is conceded as a possible source of response bias, though the source reports a wave analysis (comparing early and late responders as a non-responder proxy) that estimates the resulting bias at .11, .07 and .02 for Stress, Attitude and Resources respectively, small relative to the .18-to-.65 range of differences found between subgroups [[haven-2019a]]. The sample over-represents PhD students and women relative to the eligible population; the source reports having adjusted for gender as a confounder and found no effect modification by it, and judges on that basis that sample selectivity is unlikely to have biased its results [[haven-2019a]]. Because this is stated to be the first study using the PPQr, the source says it is rather difficult to interpret the absolute levels and differences in publication pressure it found [[haven-2019a]].

Gopalakrishna and colleagues could calculate a response rate only for the institutions that supplied email addresses (21.2%) and say they cannot assess the representativeness of their sample [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. They also note that respondents promoted within the survey's three-year window may be misclassified by rank [[gopalakrishna-2022]].
