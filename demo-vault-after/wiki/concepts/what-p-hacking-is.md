# What is p-hacking and which practices produce it?

## Definition

Head et al. treat p-hacking as one of two researcher-driven publication biases. The other is selection, or the file drawer effect, in which studies with non-significant results are published less often [[head-2015]]. P-hacking, also called inflation bias or selective reporting, is the misreporting of true effect sizes in published studies, produced when researchers try several analyses or data eligibility rules and report those that give significant results [[head-2015]]. The abstract puts it as collecting or selecting data or analyses until non-significant results become significant [[head-2015]].

Bishop and Thompson draw the same line between two distortions. Publication bias affects which studies get published; p-hacking affects which data and analyses from a single study get into the publication [[bishop-2016]]. They attribute the term to Simonsohn, Nelson and Simmons (2014) and describe the practice as reporting only the part of a dataset that yields significant results, with the choice made after looking at the data, adding that the practice was described without the name in 1956 [[bishop-2016]].

Head et al. and Bishop and Thompson agree on the definition; they differ in name, Head et al. also calling it inflation bias and Bishop and Thompson giving their own name, ghost variables, to one practice within it [[head-2015]] [[bishop-2016]].

Bruns and Ioannidis follow the notation of Simonsohn, Nelson and Simmons (2014) and define p-hacking as the selection of statistically significant estimates for publication within each study [[bruns-2016]]. The definition matches the other two in placing the selection within a study rather than between studies; what Bruns and Ioannidis add is a distinction by research design, set out below, between p-hacking that relies on chance and p-hacking that relies on a systematic bias [[bruns-2016]].

Hartgerink glosses p-hacking as statistical significance seeking and files it among questionable research practices, without a definition beyond that; the paper's interest is in which forms of it leave a mark on the distribution of reported p-values [[hartgerink-2017]].

Fraser and colleagues, surveying researchers directly about their own use of these practices rather than inferring use from published results, define p hacking as checking statistical significance before deciding whether to collect more data, stopping data collection early once significance is reached, deciding to exclude data points only after checking the impact on significance and not reporting that impact, adjusting statistical models based on the resulting strength of the effect of interest, and rounding a p-value to meet a threshold; they define cherry picking as failing to report non-significant variables, relationships, conditions or treatments, and HARKing as presenting an unexpected or exploratory finding as though predicted from the start, attributing the two senses to Kerr (1998) and Wagenmakers et al. (2012) respectively [[fraser-2018]]. These definitions match the practices the other sources count rather than adding a new construct; what the paper adds is a measurement of how often researchers report having used them, which is on [[prevalence-from-self-report]] rather than this page [[fraser-2018]].

Agnoli and colleagues, also surveying researchers directly, use no definitions of their own: their ten questionable research practices are John et al.'s (2012) original item wordings, translated into Italian for a direct replication [[agnoli-2017]]. The items are not named as cherry picking, p hacking or HARKing in the paper, but several match the constructs the other sources already use, as set out below [[agnoli-2017]].

Wicherts and colleagues approach the same subject from the choices rather than from the selection. Their term, which they attribute to Simmons et al. (2011), is researcher degrees of freedom: the choices involved in a study that are often arbitrary from a substantive or methodological point of view but that can affect the outcome of a significance test and hence the conclusions drawn [[wicherts-2016]]. They do not define p-hacking separately, describing the problem instead as the opportunistic use of these choices to obtain statistically significant results, and they distinguish two consequences of that use: an increased chance of a false positive, and inflation of effect size estimates [[wicherts-2016]]. The difference from the other four definitions is one of unit. The others define p-hacking by what the researcher selects for publication [[head-2015]] [[bishop-2016]] [[bruns-2016]]; Wicherts and colleagues enumerate the decision points at which a selection is available, whether or not anyone took it [[wicherts-2016]].

## Practices counted as p-hacking

Head et al. list: analysing data midway through an experiment to decide whether to keep collecting; recording many response variables and choosing which to report after analysis; deciding whether to drop outliers after analysis; excluding, combining or splitting treatment groups after analysis; including or excluding covariates after analysis; and stopping exploration once an analysis is significant [[head-2015]]. They also count reporting a non-significant result as "p < 0.05" as a component of p-hacking [[head-2015]].

Bishop and Thompson name deciding which outliers to exclude, when to stop collecting data, and whether to include covariates [[bishop-2016]]. Their focus is ghost variables: dependent variables measured in a study that disappear from the published paper once they turn out not to be significant [[bishop-2016]]. This is the same practice that Head et al. list as recording many response variables and deciding which to report after analysis [[head-2015]]. Bishop and Thompson also count double-dipping, choosing a region or time window by inspecting the data and then testing it, as a form of p-hacking, and note that it produces p-values well below .05 rather than just under it [[bishop-2016]].

Hartgerink names optional stopping, also called data peeking, and sorts practices by their effect on the p-value distribution: optional stopping when the null hypothesis is true can produce a surplus of p-values just below .05, whereas optional stopping when there is a true effect, or running several analyses and reporting only the one with the smallest p-value, need not, and some forms produce right skew instead [[hartgerink-2017]]. Hartgerink also counts the strategic or excessive rounding down of p-values, such as reporting p = .054 as p = .05, as p-hacking, citing Vermeulen et al. (2015) [[hartgerink-2017]]. Head et al. count a different misreporting, a non-significant result given as "p < 0.05" [[head-2015]].

For experimental research Bruns and Ioannidis list, citing Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011), choosing dependent variables or covariates after the fact, adding observations until the estimate is significant, and reporting only a subset of experimental conditions [[bruns-2016]]. They also relay from the survey by John et al. (2012) three practices that can serve p-hacking: excluding data after the fact, rounding down p-values, and framing an unexpected finding as predicted from the start [[bruns-2016]]. The rounding down of p-values is thus counted by two sources, Hartgerink from its effect on the p-value distribution and Bruns and Ioannidis from the survey literature [[hartgerink-2017]] [[bruns-2016]].

Fraser and colleagues' survey names ten practices, matching several already on this page: not reporting non-significant response variables or covariates, matching cherry picking and Bishop and Thompson's ghost variables [[fraser-2018]] [[head-2015]] [[bishop-2016]]; reporting an unexpected finding as predicted, matching HARKing as Bruns and Ioannidis relay it and Wicherts and colleagues' entry for the same practice [[fraser-2018]] [[bruns-2016]] [[wicherts-2016]]; rounding off a p-value to meet a threshold, matching Hartgerink's and Bruns and Ioannidis's entries above [[fraser-2018]] [[hartgerink-2017]] [[bruns-2016]]; excluding data points after checking significance, and collecting more data after checking significance, both matching Head et al.'s list [[fraser-2018]] [[head-2015]]; and changing to another statistical analysis after the first failed to reach significance, matching Wicherts and colleagues' choice-between-models entry [[fraser-2018]] [[wicherts-2016]]. Two of the ten are not named by the other sources on this page: reporting a subset of tested statistical models as the complete set, and not disclosing known problems in the method, analysis or data quality that could affect conclusions [[fraser-2018]]. The tenth, filling in missing data points without identifying them as simulated, is a form of fabrication rather than p-hacking as the other sources use the term, and the paper treats it separately for that reason, asking it last in the survey [[fraser-2018]].

Agnoli and colleagues' ten items, being John et al.'s (2012) own wordings, overlap with several practices already named on this page. Failing to report all of a study's dependent measures matches Head et al.'s recording-and-selecting-response-variables entry and Bishop and Thompson's ghost variables, and is the same item Fraser and colleagues' survey adapted as its own practice 1 [[agnoli-2017]] [[head-2015]] [[bishop-2016]] [[fraser-2018]]. Deciding whether to collect more data after checking significance, and stopping data collection early once a looked-for result appears, both match Head et al.'s "analysing data midway through an experiment" and Hartgerink's optional stopping [[agnoli-2017]] [[head-2015]] [[hartgerink-2017]]. Failing to report all of a study's conditions matches Bruns and Ioannidis's "reporting only a subset of experimental conditions," relayed from the same John et al. survey [[agnoli-2017]] [[bruns-2016]]. Rounding off a p-value matches Hartgerink's and Bruns and Ioannidis's entries and Fraser and colleagues' practice 5 [[agnoli-2017]] [[hartgerink-2017]] [[bruns-2016]] [[fraser-2018]]. Deciding whether to exclude data after checking its impact on significance matches Wicherts and colleagues' outlier-exclusion entry and Fraser and colleagues' practice 6 [[agnoli-2017]] [[wicherts-2016]] [[fraser-2018]]. Reporting an unexpected finding as predicted matches HARKing as named on this page already [[agnoli-2017]] [[bruns-2016]] [[wicherts-2016]] [[fraser-2018]].

Two of Agnoli and colleagues' ten items are not matched elsewhere on this page. Selectively reporting studies that "worked" sits between the p-hacking practices this page counts and the file-drawer selection that Head et al. treat as a distinct bias from p-hacking, and no other source's list distinguishes the two clearly enough to place it with confidence [[agnoli-2017]] [[head-2015]]. Claiming that results are unaffected by demographic variables when actually unsure, or knowing that they do, is not named by any other source [[agnoli-2017]]. As with Fraser and colleagues' tenth item, Agnoli and colleagues' tenth item, falsifying data, is treated by the paper itself as a distinct and more serious practice than the other nine, not properly a questionable research practice at all but outright misconduct [[agnoli-2017]].

### Where in a study the choices arise

The other sources list practices; Wicherts and colleagues organise them by the phase of the study in which the choice is made, and give 34 of them across five phases: 2 in hypothesizing, 7 in design, 4 in data collection, 15 in analysis and 6 in reporting [[wicherts-2016]]. Their point in separating design from analysis is that the analytic flexibility the other sources describe is created earlier, when a study is built with redundancy in it, so that six of their design entries are paired with a later choice, five with an analysis choice and one with the stopping rule in data collection, and a seventh pair links exploratory hypothesising to the reporting stage [[wicherts-2016]]. Measuring the same dependent variable in several ways pairs with selecting one of them; measuring additional constructs pairs with promoting one to primary outcome; measuring extra variables pairs with choosing which to enter as covariates, mediators or moderators; measuring awareness or manipulation checks pairs with using them as exclusion criteria; creating multiple manipulated variables and conditions pairs with selecting or recombining them; and failing to specify a sampling plan pairs with setting the stopping rule by intermediate testing [[wicherts-2016]].

Several of their entries name practices the other sources already count: choosing whether to include covariates, deciding how to deal with outliers, setting the stopping rule on the basis of intermediate significance testing, selecting the dependent variable out of several measures, and misreporting results and p-values [[wicherts-2016]] [[head-2015]] [[bishop-2016]] [[hartgerink-2017]]. Their entry for presenting exploratory analyses as confirmatory, HARKing, matches the practice Bruns and Ioannidis relay from the survey literature as framing an unexpected finding as predicted from the start [[wicherts-2016]] [[bruns-2016]].

Others are on no other source's list. In the design phase: failing to conduct a well-founded power analysis, and failing to specify a sampling plan, which allows several small studies to be run and only the best presented [[wicherts-2016]]. In the collection phase: failing to randomly assign participants to conditions, insufficient blinding of participants or experimenters, and correcting, coding or discarding data during collection in a non-blinded manner [[wicherts-2016]]. In the analysis phase: ad hoc handling of missing data, of pre-processing, and of violations of statistical assumptions; trying different ways of scoring the chosen dependent variable, which they argue is possible even when the scale is named in advance; operationalising independent variables in different ways; choosing between statistical models; choosing the estimation method, software package and computation of standard errors; and choosing inference criteria such as the alpha level, the sidedness of the test and corrections for multiple testing [[wicherts-2016]]. In the reporting phase: failing to assure reproducibility, failing to enable replication, failing to mention or misrepresenting a preregistration, and failing to report studies deemed relevant at the outset but not published [[wicherts-2016]].

Their argument for why the smaller choices matter is that using them to obtain significance is more effective with smaller samples, since sampling variability is larger and each decision has a proportionately larger effect, so underpowered studies are themselves more susceptible to bias [[wicherts-2016]]. Their illustration of how quickly the options multiply is a single experimental factor with three conditions, which they say already yields seven operationalisations for the analysis [[wicherts-2016]].

### P-hacking in observational research

Bruns and Ioannidis argue that observational research, meaning any study without randomisation in the comparison of interest, has an additional and, on their account, major source of p-hacking [[bruns-2016]]. When regression coefficients are estimated from observational data, the researcher chooses the functional form, the estimation technique and, above all, the set of adjusting variables meant to control for confounders, and this flexibility yields a wide range of estimates from which significant ones can be selected; the paper says the practice is also known as multiple modelling, data snooping or data-mining and can cause what has been called a vibration of effects [[bruns-2016]].

The mechanism is omitted-variable bias: leaving out a variable that affects the outcome and is correlated with the variable of interest biases the estimate, as when exam grades are regressed on class attendance without adjusting for ability or study effort [[bruns-2016]]. Bruns and Ioannidis hold that this differs in kind from the experimental practices above. Under randomisation estimation stays unbiased and p-hacking relies on chance; omitted-variable bias makes estimation biased and inconsistent, so the p-value approaches zero as the sample grows whether or not a true effect exists, and even a tiny bias suffices with a large enough sample [[bruns-2016]]. Other biases that also make estimation inconsistent, namely simultaneity, misspecification of the functional form and measurement error, are named as working the same way [[bruns-2016]]. They add that the same flexibility reaches nominally experimental studies whose randomisation has been subverted, and that adjusted and unadjusted analyses of the same trial can reach different conclusions [[bruns-2016]].

Bruns and Ioannidis concede that omitting a confounder is not necessarily p-hacking: it may reflect unavailable data or not knowing what to adjust for, and even experts rarely agree on the adjustment set [[bruns-2016]]. For what this mechanism does to the distribution of published p-values, see [[p-curve-as-evidence]].

## How much ghost variables inflate false positives

Bishop and Thompson give the arithmetic: comparing two groups on ten independent variables with no true difference gives a probability of .401 that at least one differs at p < .05, so a researcher who reports only the significant one has a 40% chance of a false positive [[bishop-2016]]. In their simulations of ghost p-hacking under a null effect, the false positive rate was around 40% when the variables were uncorrelated and around 12% when they were inter-correlated at r = .8, and it rose with the number of variables measured (8 against 3) [[bishop-2016]]. They say that if all variables were reported, reviewers might require a correction for multiple comparisons, and that it is the disappearance of the non-significant variables that makes the published result misleading [[bishop-2016]].

## Incentives said to drive it

Head et al. attribute p-hacking to the incentive to publish significant results, which they trace to journals disproportionately publishing significant findings and to employers and funders assessing researchers by paper counts weighted by journal impact factor [[head-2015]]. They argue that many practices leading to p-hacking are still regarded as acceptable by researchers, citing a survey of psychologists (John et al., 2012), and report an anonymous reviewer's claim that some animal ethics boards encourage or mandate stopping a study once a significant result is obtained [[head-2015]]. They judge that eliminating p-hacking is unlikely while careers are assessed by publication output and publication decisions depend on statistical support [[head-2015]].

Bishop and Thompson say both publication bias and p-hacking can arise when scientists are reluctant to write up unexciting results or when editors are biased against them, and place the wider problem in a gap between what statistical software allows and what researchers understand about statistics [[bishop-2016]]. They add that ghost variables are not always recognised by researchers as a problem [[bishop-2016]].

Bruns and Ioannidis say there is evidence that the incentive system of academic publishing fosters scientists to engage in questionable research practices in search of significant results, citing the survey by John et al. (2012) and a meta-analysis of misconduct surveys by Fanelli (2009), and do not go further into causes [[bruns-2016]].

Agnoli and colleagues argue that the consistency of self-admission rates across the US, Italian and German surveys they compare points to systemic causes rather than to national or individual failings: competitive pressure to publish, editors and reviewers favouring statistically significant results, and researcher choices that these pressures reward [[agnoli-2017]]. They add a national-level illustration, the bibliometric criteria of the Italian National Scientific Qualification introduced in 2010, as an example of an evaluation system that rewards publication counts and citation metrics and so motivates the same choices [[agnoli-2017]]. This account sits with Head et al.'s, Bishop and Thompson's and Bruns and Ioannidis's incentive-to-publish framing rather than with Wicherts and colleagues' arbitrary-choice framing, described next [[agnoli-2017]] [[head-2015]] [[bishop-2016]] [[bruns-2016]].

Wicherts and colleagues say little about incentives and instead question whether the practices need a motive at all. They report sympathy with Gelman and Loken's argument that the term questionable research practices is not always apt here, because the majority of the choices they list are arbitrary ones that a researcher simply has to make and could, but will not necessarily, use opportunistically [[wicherts-2016]]. What matters on their account is not that all researchers try to obtain desirable results but that the data could have been collected and analysed differently, and that the analyses finally reported could have been chosen differently had the results come out differently [[wicherts-2016]]. They add that the potential for bias is particularly severe for experiments studying subtle effects with relatively small samples [[wicherts-2016]]. The other sources treat the practices as motivated by the incentive to publish significant results [[head-2015]] [[bishop-2016]] [[bruns-2016]] [[agnoli-2017]]; no source replies to the other's framing, and the difference is set out on [[preregistration-as-a-remedy]].

Every source above asserts or infers the incentive to publish from its own results or from citing other work; none of them measures the pressure to publish directly with a validated instrument. Haven, Bouter and colleagues do, surveying 1,073 Amsterdam academic researchers with the revised Publication Pressure Questionnaire (PPQr) and finding a substantial negative attitude toward the current publication climate in the sample overall, together with rank and field differences in perceived publication stress and in the resources available to manage it [[haven-2019a]]. This measures how researchers perceive the pressure to publish, a construct related to but not the same as the incentive structure the sources above name (journal preference for significant findings, assessment by publication metrics), and it is not a measurement of any p-hacking practice: the paper reports no data on p-hacking, cherry picking, HARKing or any other questionable research practice, and its PPQr instrument is a different construct from the self-admission and estimated-colleague-use items the two surveys on [[prevalence-from-self-report]] use, so its figures are not merged with theirs. Full figures are on [[publication-pressure-by-rank-and-field]].

Gopalakrishna and colleagues measure perceived publication pressure and practice use in the same respondents, 6,813 academic researchers in the Netherlands, and find each standard deviation of publication pressure associated with 1.22 times the odds (95% CI 1.14 to 1.30) of frequently engaging in at least one of 11 questionable research practices over the last three years [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The paper reads this as support for changing the "publish or perish" reward system [[gopalakrishna-2022]]. The association is cross-sectional, and the 11 practices are mostly not the p-hacking practices named on this page: the nearest are not submitting valid negative studies for publication, which is closer to the file-drawer selection Head et al. treat as distinct from p-hacking, and failing to report important study details in publications [[gopalakrishna-2022]] [[head-2015]]. Details are on [[factors-associated-with-qrp-use]].

## Remedies proposed

Head et al. put education of researchers first [[head-2015]]. For researchers they recommend labelling studies as prespecified or exploratory, adhering to common analysis standards, measuring only response variables known or predicted to matter, using sufficient samples, analysing data blind where possible, and assessing methods independently of results [[head-2015]]. For journals they recommend full reporting guidelines (effect sizes whether small or large, p-values to three decimal places, sample sizes, the entire analysis process), platforms for prespecification, and open access to raw data, which they say does not prevent p-hacking but makes researchers accountable for marginal results [[head-2015]].

Bishop and Thompson say the solutions are likely to require both changes in the training of scientists in methods and revision of the incentive structure of science, and name pre-registration of protocols and analyses as a recommended solution to publication bias that scientists have been slow to adopt [[bishop-2016]]. Their paper is about detection rather than remedy and does not go further than this [[bishop-2016]].

Bruns and Ioannidis address observational research. For studies that test hypotheses they recommend careful pre-specification of the analysis plan, citing a survey by Boccia et al. (2015) as finding that registered observational protocols almost never pre-specify their statistical analysis [[bruns-2016]]. For hypothesis-generating studies without a pre-specified plan they ask that the exploratory character be reported transparently, that results obtained under different models be acknowledged, and that the model chosen for emphasis be interpreted with great caution [[bruns-2016]]. They also raise the empirical calibration of p-values against an empirical null distribution built from comparisons believed to have no causal link, citing Schuemie et al. (2014), and suggest that research on large observational datasets should not judge p-values against theoretical null distributions and the 0.05 threshold, while conceding that calibration is not always possible because a sufficient set of uncontested true positives and true negatives may not exist [[bruns-2016]]. Head et al.'s recommendation that studies be labelled prespecified or exploratory [[head-2015]] and Bishop and Thompson's endorsement of pre-registration [[bishop-2016]] point the same way as the pre-specification that Bruns and Ioannidis recommend, though each source states it for its own field.

Wicherts and colleagues take the same remedy further than the others by asking what a preregistration would have to contain in order to close off each of the choices on their list, and offer the list itself as a checklist for assessing preregistrations and for gauging the potential for bias in studies that were never registered [[wicherts-2016]]. Where a study is not registered they suggest reporting all potentially relevant analyses, as sensitivity analyses or as a multiverse analysis, or making the data available for independent reanalysis [[wicherts-2016]]. Their requirements for a preregistration, and how the six sources' recommendations compare, are on [[preregistration-as-a-remedy]].
