# Perceived publication pressure in Amsterdam: Survey of all disciplinary fields and academic ranks

## Research question and definition

The paper's research question is the level of perceived publication pressure across the four academic institutions in Amsterdam, and whether it differs by academic rank and disciplinary field. Perceived publication pressure is defined, following Woolf (1986), as the subjective pressure resulting from the feeling that one has to publish; drawing on work stress literature, the paper reasons that a strong perception of pressure need not produce stress when the person has resources available to manage it. The paper states that some publication pressure can incentivise high-quality work, but that too much is associated (citing other work, not restated here) with poor-quality research, reduced willingness to share raw data, less involvement in public and policy issues, less academic creativity, and a disproportionate focus on positive and "spectacular" findings; it also cites prior work finding junior researchers to experience more publication pressure than senior researchers, and states that earlier studies were confined to particular disciplines and a subset of academic ranks, which this study's design is meant to address.

## Participants and procedure

All academic researchers employed for at least one day per week at one of four Amsterdam institutions (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, University of Amsterdam, and the two Amsterdam University Medical Centers), including PhD students (who are employees in the Netherlands), were eligible. A data-sharing agreement with the institutions supplied email addresses; 7,548 researchers were sent an information letter and, a week later, invited by email to an online Qualtrics survey (about 15 minutes), with three reminders 10 days apart. The survey recorded academic rank (PhD student, postdoc, assistant professor, associate professor, full professor) and disciplinary field (biomedicine: life and medical sciences; natural sciences; social sciences: social and behavioural sciences; humanities: humanities, language, communication, law and arts). The study was ethically approved by the Faculty of Behavioural and Movement Sciences (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam) review board and preregistered on the Open Science Framework (osf.io/w4t7u).

## Instrument

Publication pressure was measured with the revised Publication Pressure Questionnaire (PPQr), a validated instrument with three subscales scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = totally disagree, 5 = totally agree), each subscale score the average of its items: Publication Stress (6 items, Cronbach's alpha = .804), the stress a researcher feels from having to publish; Publication Attitude (6 items, alpha = .777), the researcher's attitude toward the current publication climate; and Publication Resources (6 items, alpha = .754), factors that can help prevent publication pressure, such as feeling of competence or supportive colleagues. A higher score on every subscale indicates more perceived stress, a more negative attitude, and fewer perceived resources respectively. Two further instruments (a research climate survey and a 60-item research misbehaviours inventory) were included in the same survey but are reported in separate papers, named below.

## Statistical method

Analyses were preregistered on the OSF. The paper first computed overall and stratified (by rank and by field) mean subscale scores; second, tested pairwise differences between ranks and between fields with Bonferroni-corrected F-tests and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals; third, fitted multivariable regression models of rank and field on the subscale scores, checking for confounding and interaction, with confounder-adjusted estimates reported where applicable. A subsequent effect-size analysis (below) was explicitly not preregistered and is flagged by the paper as exploratory.

## Response rate

Of 7,548 invitees, 92 (1%) bounced or asked to be unsubscribed and 146 (2%) instead completed a short non-response questionnaire; these figures are taken from the paper's response-rate flowchart (Fig 1), which the body text corroborates only for the 2% figure. Of the invitees, 2,274 (30%) opened the questionnaire; of those, 1,201 (16% of the original 7,548) stopped before reaching the PPQr, leaving 1,073 (14%) who completed it. Demographic composition of the 1,073 completers (Table 1): 441 male, 632 female; 503 PhD students, 318 postdocs and assistant professors, 216 associate and full professors (36 did not disclose rank); 603 biomedicine, 119 natural sciences, 242 social sciences, 109 humanities.

## Overall subscale scores

Across the full sample, the highest mean subscale score was Attitude (M = 3.59), followed by Stress (M = 3.22) and Resources (M = 2.21), indicating a substantial negative attitude toward the current publication climate, a somewhat lesser degree of publication stress, and a comparatively small perceived lack of resources. Full stratified means and SDs by gender, rank and field are given in the paper's Table 1 and are not all reproduced here; the two figures the abstract highlights are Stress M = 3.42 (SD .74) for postdocs and assistant professors, the highest-stress rank group, and Resources M = 2.44 (SD .61) for PhD students, who report the greatest perceived lack of resources. Pairwise comparisons behind both figures are given below under "Differences by academic rank."

## Differences by academic rank

Pairwise Bonferroni-corrected, confounder-adjusted (disciplinary field and gender) mean differences (MD, with 95% CI) between ranks, read from Fig 2's lettered brackets since the accompanying legend text is corrupted in the extraction and the letter-to-pair mapping is only shown in the figure image itself (marked as taken from the figure):

- Stress: PhD students vs postdocs/assistant professors, MD = .237, CI [.103, .371]; postdocs/assistant professors vs associate/full professors, MD = .384, CI [.219, .549].
- Attitude: PhD students vs associate/full professors, MD = .181, CI [.049, .314]; postdocs/assistant professors vs associate/full professors, MD = .282, CI [.139, .426].
- Resources: PhD students vs postdocs/assistant professors, MD = .322, CI [.223, .421]; PhD students vs associate/full professors, MD = .645, CI [.532, .757]; postdocs/assistant professors vs associate/full professors, MD = .322, CI [.201, .444].

In words, the paper reports: postdocs and assistant professors perceive significantly more publication stress than both PhD students and associate/full professors; PhD students and postdocs/assistant professors both have a more negative attitude than associate/full professors; PhD students perceive significantly fewer resources than every other rank; and postdocs/assistant professors perceive fewer resources than associate/full professors. Full pairwise and regression tables are held in supporting information (S1-S2 Tables) not part of the extraction.

## Differences by disciplinary field

Pairwise Bonferroni-corrected, confounder-adjusted (academic rank and gender) mean differences between fields, again read from Fig 3's lettered brackets for the same reason (marked as taken from the figure):

- Stress: biomedicine vs humanities, MD = .297, CI [.080, .515]; biomedicine vs social sciences, MD = .202, CI [.042, .368]; natural sciences vs humanities, MD = .318, CI [.040, .596].
- Resources: biomedicine vs natural sciences, MD = .204, CI [.048, .359]; social sciences vs natural sciences, MD = .210, CI [.036, .384].

In words: humanities researchers perceive more publication stress than both biomedicine and natural sciences; social sciences researchers perceive more stress than biomedicine; there were no statistically significant field differences on Attitude; and biomedicine and social sciences researchers both perceive significantly fewer resources than natural sciences researchers.

## Effect modification

The paper found effect modification by disciplinary field only for the rank differences in Resources: differences between PhD students and senior researchers in perceived Resources were larger in the natural sciences than in the other fields. The paper's Table 2 gives Resources means stratified by rank within field: natural sciences 2.36 (PhD) / 1.99 (postdoc/assistant professor) / 1.44 (associate/full professor); biomedicine 2.44 / 2.16 / 1.81; social sciences 2.54 / 2.07 / 1.84; humanities 2.33 / 2.25 / 1.98.

## Effect sizes

Because 12 significant pairwise differences were found across many tests, the paper calculated effect sizes (Hedges' g, interpreted on Cohen's 1995 conventions: .20 small, .50 medium, .80 large, 1.30 very large) to guide which differences merit discussion; this analysis was explicitly not preregistered and is flagged as exploratory. From the paper's Table 3: Stress, PhD students vs postdocs/assistant professors g = .31 (small), postdocs/assistant professors vs associate/full professors g = .50 (medium); Attitude, associate/full professors vs PhD students g = .15 (small), associate/full professors vs postdocs/assistant professors g = .41 (small); Resources, postdocs/assistant professors vs PhD students g = .55 (medium), associate/full professors vs PhD students g = 1.09 (very large), associate/full professors vs postdocs/assistant professors g = .59 (medium); Stress, biomedicine vs humanities g = .33 (small), biomedicine vs social sciences g = .20 (small), natural sciences vs humanities g = .38 (small); Resources, natural sciences vs biomedicine g = .32 (small), natural sciences vs social sciences g = .31 (small). The paper states it focuses further discussion on differences of medium effect size or above.

## The paper's own interpretation

Postdocs and assistant professors are read as perceiving the most stress and the most negative attitude because this group is pursuing a (tenured) position under promotion criteria that are largely quantitative publication metrics, while associate and full professors, having an established position, perceive less pressure; PhD students' thesis defence is not usually contingent on publication count, which the paper offers as a reason their stress is somewhat lower, alongside the possibility that some PhD students do not intend to pursue an academic career. PhD students nonetheless perceive the greatest lack of resources, which the paper reads as both alarming and understandable given their relative inexperience, and recommends that junior researchers, PhD students and starting postdocs alike, would benefit most from supportive colleagues and supervisors, noting that mentoring may currently be suboptimal (citing other work, not restated here). Field differences were significant but small; natural sciences researchers perceived the most resources, which the paper attributes to the field's typical organisation into large collaborative research teams, though PhD students within natural sciences perceived a similar lack of resources to PhD students in other fields, which the paper attributes to possibly insufficient mentoring in the publication process specifically for that group.

## Limitations the paper concedes

The completion rate was 14%, which the paper concedes may indicate response bias, while noting this rate is comparable to other web-based surveys (citing other work). Only 2% of invitees completed the non-response questionnaire, too few, the paper states, to assess whether non-responders differed from responders; the paper speculates two possible sources of bias in opposite directions (over-focus on their own publications, or discomfort given public debate about publication culture in the Netherlands) without resolving which, if either, dominates. Comparing sample composition to population figures, the paper found a small overrepresentation of biomedicine (56% of completers vs 53% of invitees at the medical centres) and larger overrepresentation of PhD students (41% of the sample vs 30% of the workforce) and of women (57% of the sample vs 44% of researchers in Amsterdam); the paper reports having corrected for gender in its confounder-adjusted estimates and found no effect modification by gender, and concludes on that basis that it is unlikely the sample's selectivity biased its results. A wave analysis (late responders as a proxy for non-responders, following Phillips et al., 2016) found differences of .13, .07 and .02 (Stress, Attitude, Resources) between early and late responders; multiplied by the 86% non-response proportion, this gives an estimated non-response bias of .11, .07 and .02, which the paper judges small relative to the .18-to-.65 range of differences it found between subgroups, and concludes non-response is unlikely to have affected its conclusions.

The paper also concedes that the PPQr measures publication pressure specifically and cannot separate how much of a researcher's overall pressure is due to publishing as against other professional duties (teaching, other role conflicts), and that this is an open question it has not itself studied systematically. It names broader neoliberal and "Taylorist" reforms to universities as a candidate context for publication pressure, explicitly stating that a full review of this literature is beyond the paper's scope and directing the reader to another paper (Lorenz, cited but not restated here) for that discussion. Finally, because this is stated to be the first study using the PPQr, the paper says it is rather difficult to interpret the absolute levels and differences in publication pressure it found.

## Future research the paper proposes

The paper suggests testing whether the rank and field differences generalise internationally, citing a single prior finding (with an earlier version of the PPQ, not the PPQr used here) that Flemish biomedical researchers reported more publication pressure than Dutch colleagues; that finding is the cited paper's own and is not verified or restated here as a result of this study. It also proposes a longitudinal study of whether publication pressure is associated with burnout and research misbehaviour, and a qualitative study of what high publication pressure means for researchers and how it affects their work.

The same survey also administered the Survey of Organizational Research Climate and a 60-item research misbehaviours inventory; the paper states that analyses of those instruments, and of how they interrelate with publication pressure, are reported in separate papers.
